ChainRecover Whitepaper — v1.2

Blockchain Forensics & Asset Recovery Methodology

Abstract

Public blockchains are permanent ledgers, not banks. Once a transaction is confirmed it cannot be reversed by any central authority — yet value is rarely truly gone; it is only moved. This paper describes the methodology ChainRecover uses to follow that movement, attribute it to real-world entities, and convert a technical trace into evidence that exchanges, insurers and courts will accept.

1. The Irreversibility Problem

Crypto-asset losses fall into two categories: unauthorised transfers (theft, phishing, exploits) and accidental transfers (wrong address, wrong network, lost keys). Neither is reversible at the protocol level. Recovery is therefore not a technical “undo” — it is an investigative and legal process that depends on where the assets ultimately land.

2. Why the Chain Is the Evidence

Every transfer leaves a permanent, timestamped, cryptographically verifiable record. Unlike a bank statement, the record cannot be edited by any party. This gives forensic analysts an unusually strong evidentiary base — provided the analysis is reproducible and the chain of custody is documented.

3. The Six-Layer Tracing Model

  • Layer 1 — Transaction graph: every input and output for every address in scope, reconstructed block by block.
  • Layer 2 — Temporal analysis: timing, gas price and nonce behaviour used to group addresses controlled by one actor.
  • Layer 3 — Clustering heuristics: common-input ownership, change-address detection, dusting patterns and exchange deposit tagging.
  • Layer 4 — Cross-chain resolution: bridge deposits matched to withdrawals on the destination chain, including wrapped and canonical assets.
  • Layer 5 — Off-ramp detection: identification of centralised exchange, payment processor or OTC desk endpoints.
  • Layer 6 — Evidence packaging: hashes, timestamps, screenshots and signed analyst statements compiled into a court-ready report.

4. Wallet Clustering Heuristics

Attribution relies on probabilistic signals, not certainty. We combine multiple weak signals — shared funding sources, identical gas behaviour, sequential nonces, reused deposit addresses and known-label databases — into a single confidence score per cluster. Clusters below a 0.72 confidence threshold are never presented as fact in a report.

5. Bridges and Cross-Chain Movement

Bridges break naïve tracing. We resolve them by matching deposit value, timing and destination address on the far side, and by treating wrapped assets as claims on the underlying asset rather than as separate currencies. Where a bridge cannot be resolved deterministically, the report states this explicitly.

7. Evidence & Compliance Standards

Reports are written to a standard usable by counsel, insurers and law enforcement: every claim is traceable to a transaction hash, every hash is verifiable on a public explorer, and every analytical step is reproducible by a second analyst. Client data is encrypted at rest and covered by a mutual NDA.

8. Limitations & Honest Caveats

No firm can guarantee recovery. Outcomes depend on the cooperation of exchanges, the speed of law enforcement, and the actions of the wrongdoer. We decline cases we believe are not recoverable, and we say so before any fee is agreed. Any service that guarantees recovery, or that requests a seed phrase, should be treated as fraudulent.

9. Conclusion

The blockchain does not forget. With disciplined tracing, careful attribution and well-prepared evidence, a meaningful proportion of lost assets can be located and — through the cooperation of regulated intermediaries — returned. That is the entire premise of ChainRecover.
